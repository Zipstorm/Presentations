The short version

Provn is a pre-revenue, self-funded Seattle startup founded by serial entrepreneur Nikesh (Niki) Parekh — the same founder who built and sold Suplari to Microsoft in 2021. It's a challenge-based hiring platform pitched as "the AI Talent Draft": instead of applying with a resume, candidates apply by completing a real, employer-designed work sample, recording a video walkthrough of their thinking, and documenting their AI usage. Hiring managers then review the work directly, scored against an explicit rubric.

The thesis is current and sharp: every 2026 job posting now gets 500–1,000 applications, every resume looks AI-polished, and the only signal that survives is demonstrated work plus thinking process. Provn's tagline — "Performance over pedigree. Proof over polish." — is a direct attack on credential-based screening and on resume-optimization tools. Notably, Provn explicitly tells candidates to use AI on challenges, not hide it; the differentiator is the video walkthrough of how they used it.

Why this matters for Helix: Provn and Helix are pointed at the same candidate pain (AI has made resumes useless; candidates can't break through) but solving it from opposite sides. Helix produces a new candidate-owned portfolio artifact distinct from a resume or LinkedIn. Provn argues the resume should be replaced and is building the replacement on the employer side. Both are betting on a new portfolio artifact replacing the resume — the interesting differences are where the artifact lives and what fills it.

§ 03What Provn actually does

Provn is structured as a two-sided marketplace between candidates and employers, with the work sample ("challenge") as the central artifact. The event format is called the AI Talent Draft. Challenges are drawn from the real domain of each role — diagnosing a churn spike before a board meeting, untangling conflicting metrics for a dashboard, fixing silent order failures in an e-commerce pipeline, defining an AI-native fan platform for a league owner dinner. Each challenge has its own scenario, customer, and constraints.

The candidate experience

  1. Sign up and set up a profile. Resume upload, profile basics, and up to 5 target roles from a curated taxonomy (Engineering, Product & Design, Data & AI, Operations, Sales, Marketing, Finance, People, Administration).
  2. Browse jobs. Currently ~22 live jobs from named employers like Envorso Sports, DAT Freight & Analytics, Arrivia, Golden Analytics, mpathic, Gordian Software, Collabera, and Brilliant Earth — mostly Seattle / PNW, with explicit comp bands, apply-by deadlines, and a marker showing which roles require a challenge.
  3. Apply by completing a challenge. Each role has its own custom challenge — typically 50–120 minutes — with full context, a scoring rubric exposed up front, and three artifacts to submit: a written brief, a README with a mandatory AI Usage Log, and a 5–7 minute video walkthrough answering a mandatory AI question: "Walk me through one moment where you disagreed with, pushed back on, or redirected what the AI gave you."
  4. Get reviewed by a real person. Per Provn's marketing: "every completed application is reviewed by a real person… not a keyword filter, not a bot." Top performers get surfaced directly to the hiring manager, bypassing recruiter screening.
  5. Build a public profile that travels. Every completed challenge becomes a reusable portfolio artifact attached to a public, shareable candidate profile at provn.co/u/{username} — clean username-based URLs, with a preview mode so candidates can see exactly what employers will see. This is functionally the LinkedIn analogue in Provn's worldview: a single canonical page that aggregates challenge submissions, video walkthroughs, and selected role interests.

Beyond the per-job challenges, Provn also runs an AI Talent Draft — a parallel library of standalone challenges (Diagnose a Churn Spike, Untangle Conflicting Metrics, Diagnose Silent Order Failures, etc.) organized by function. These serve double duty: practice problems for candidates building their portfolio, and a recruiting funnel for employers who can scout high performers from the leaderboard.

The employer experience (inferred)

  • Challenges are co-designed with the hiring manager before the role goes live (per the Spring 2026 Talent Draft launch post). This is explicit positioning — they want candidates to know a human designed the challenge so they trust a human will read the response.
  • Submissions arrive as a packaged portfolio: brief + README + video, with the AI usage log front and center.
  • Hiring managers — explicitly not recruiters — review work. Parekh's stated success metric: candidate submits → VP / hiring manager interviews directly.
  • The Envorso Founding PM challenge I reviewed used five weighted categories: Founding PM Craft & 0→1 Execution (30%), AI-Native Product & Builder Mindset (20%), Strategic Product & Data Platform Thinking (18%), Customer & Stakeholder Presence (18%), AI Fluency (14%). Rubric is published before the candidate starts.

The challenge construction is the product

This is the part worth studying closely. The Envorso challenge isn't a generic "build something cool" prompt. It includes:

  • A realistic founding scenario (9-person startup, named CEO, named teammates, named first customer).
  • Explicit constraints designed to test judgment: "You can't hire a Staff Engineer this quarter. The fan you're designing for is 52, not 25. The audience for your video is non-technical sports executives."
  • A weighted, published rubric.
  • A required AI usage log forcing candidates to document where they pushed back on the model.
  • A scoring framework that explicitly rewards iteration and critical evaluation of AI output over polished final answers.

The design intent is unmistakable: it's hard to fake this with a single Claude prompt, because the rubric specifically scores the process of working with AI, not the output.

"It's very hard to fake the video walkthrough. You can have AI create the transcript and read it, but when you see hundreds of people doing the same thing, the copy-and-paste-from-AI is actually pretty obvious."
— Niki Parekh, Talent Scout Webinar

The roadmap: Career Services

Surfaced via a modal during signup, Provn has a "Career Services" suite explicitly in roadmap mode ("Here's what's coming"):

  • AI Interview Prep — company-specific mock interviews scored by AI
  • Resume & LinkedIn Optimization — AI + expert recruiter review
  • Career Coaching — 1:1 with industry practitioners
  • Portfolio & Video Coaching — feedback on challenge submissions and walkthroughs
  • Job Matching — an AI career agent that finds and tailors applications
  • Skill Gap Analysis — based on Provn challenge scores

This is the layer most relevant to Helix. Today these are aspirational; the launch product is the challenge marketplace. But the direction of travel is clear: monetize candidates with premium career services on top of the free challenge platform, while monetizing employers per hire.

§ 04Business model

Per the GeekWire launch piece, the model has two revenue streams:

  1. Employer side: charge per hire (success-based, not subscription).
  2. Candidate side: premium tools, including an "AI agent" that helps candidates market themselves and find opportunities. Confirmed in the candidate FAQ: "Browsing jobs, applying via challenges, and building your portfolio is 100% free forever. Premium services will be added in the future."

Target customers

  • Large enterprises hiring early- and mid-career information workers at volume, who want a vetted candidate database.
  • Smaller startups without recruiting teams who need pre-vetted candidates with demonstrated AI fluency.

Target candidates

Lifted directly from Parekh's Talent Scout webinar — this is explicit ICP positioning, not paraphrased:

  • Candidates early in career who are strong but invisible on paper
  • International students on a US master's program
  • Career changers
  • People later in career watching AI reshape their field
  • Candidates from non-traditional backgrounds who are great with AI

Provn explicitly acknowledges they're not for everyone: "Some candidates aren't going to spend an hour completing a challenge, and that's a fair choice."

§ 05Market positioning

Provn frames itself against three categories of incumbent:

Incumbent typeHow Provn positions against it
Resume-based job boards (LinkedIn, Indeed) Massive scale but unable to separate signal from "AI slop" — every resume now looks perfect.
Technical assessment tools (HackerRank, Codility, Karat) "Narrow" — code-only, not the full job, and don't capture thinking or communication.
Resume-optimization tools Wrong abstraction. Optimizing resumes makes the AI-slop problem worse, not better.

The narrative reads as a deliberate inversion of the resume-optimization category: rather than helping candidates package themselves better for a broken system, Provn argues for replacing the system with one where packaging matters less and demonstrated work matters more.

A subtle but important positioning choice: most hiring tools right now try to detect AI use and penalize it. Provn does the opposite. "We want you to use AI. We want you to lean into it." The bet is that AI fluency is a hiring signal, not contraband — and that how you use AI is observable in the video walkthrough even when the written output is AI-assisted.

Messaging contradiction worth flagging. Provn's positioning says "we're against resume optimization." Provn's roadmap includes resume and LinkedIn optimization as a paid candidate service. The most charitable reading: they see resume optimization as table-stakes monetization for candidates who still have to operate in the rest of the job market, while the challenge marketplace is the bet that changes the system over time.

§ 07Direct relevance to Helix

This is the section worth the most attention. Provn's surface area overlaps with Helix in five specific places.

The AI-forward candidate thesis is shared — but operationalization differs

Both Helix and Provn assume that the candidate of 2026 has to demonstrate AI fluency, not claim it. Where they diverge is how that proof gets produced:

Helix's model

Produce a new candidate-owned portfolio artifact — one Helix curates from the candidate's own work history and AI-fluency signals — that the candidate then shares directly with employers. The artifact, not a resume or LinkedIn profile, is the thing being optimized.

Provn's model

The proof is a work sample completed on Provn's platform, with the AI-usage log as a structured artifact and a video answering "where did you push back on the AI?" as the highest-signal indicator. The artifact lives on Provn's public profile.

Both products bet on a new portfolio artifact replacing the resume. The differences are in where the artifact lives (candidate-owned in Helix; platform-owned in Provn) and what fills it (curated existing work in Helix; net-new challenge submissions in Provn).

Worth noting: the language Provn's employer guests use is identical to what we'd want our candidates to be able to speak. Ganesh (Arrivia): "Curious and resilient." Neal (BCG): "Work hard, accuracy, teams, curiosity, flexibility." Both explicitly say a resume can't prove any of it. This is exactly the gap both Helix and Provn are trying to close.

The Career Services roadmap is the closest direct overlap

The features Provn has explicitly listed as "coming" — AI Interview Prep, Resume & LinkedIn Optimization, Career Coaching, Portfolio & Video Coaching, Job Matching, Skill Gap Analysis — are roughly the SeekOut.ai / Helix surface area. None are live yet. Provn has explicitly opted to ship the challenge marketplace first and layer career services on top later.

This is a useful sequencing data point. Provn's bet is that the proof is the wedge, and career services are downstream monetization on top of an established candidate base. Helix's bet is closer to the inverse: the polish is the wedge, with proof a possible extension. Worth a conversation about which sequence is right for SeekOut's audience.

The AI Usage Log + Video Walkthrough is the strongest borrowable idea

The single sharpest product idea on Provn is the combination of:

  • A mandatory AI Usage Log inside the README submission, and
  • A mandatory AI question in the video walkthrough — "walk me through one moment where you disagreed with, pushed back on, or redirected the AI."

Together these produce something neither a resume nor a code sample can: credible evidence of how a candidate actually works with AI, not just whether they used it. Per Parekh, the video walkthrough is the part that's hardest to fake. That's a defensibility argument that I think mostly holds up.

For Helix specifically: this concept is directly portable as a candidate-side coaching mechanic, decoupled from Provn's marketplace. We could help candidates produce a personal AI Usage Log across their recent work — what they asked AI for, what they kept, what they rejected, and why — as a portable portfolio artifact independent of any specific application. Plus a structured "AI moment" video they could attach to LinkedIn or send with applications. A much stronger AI-fluency signal than "lists ChatGPT under skills."

The challenge rubric design is worth studying

Even setting aside the marketplace mechanics, the way Provn structures a challenge is instructive:

  • Scenario-rich and constraint-heavy, not a blank prompt.
  • Rubric is published before the candidate starts.
  • Process (iteration, redirection of AI) is explicitly weighted alongside output.
  • Submission has three distinct surfaces: written analysis, structured README, video.

If Helix ever moves into producing practice or assessment artifacts for candidates (mock challenges, portfolio prompts, "show your AI work" templates), this rubric design is a strong reference template.

The public profile is the artifact, not the resume

Provn ships every candidate a public, shareable profile at provn.co/u/{username} that aggregates their challenge work — submissions, videos, role interests — into one canonical page. This is the part most directly competitive with the LinkedIn-as-default-portfolio model that Helix is also operating against. Provn's bet is that the profile is the application: a candidate hands an employer their provn.co/u/... URL and the employer sees demonstrated work plus AI-fluency artifacts inline, not a resume that gestures at them.

Helix's canonical artifact is also a portfolio — a candidate-owned page produced only through Helix, distinct from a resume or LinkedIn profile. That puts Helix and Provn on the same side of the artifact question (a new thing the candidate hands to an employer, not a polished version of an old one) and points to direct positioning comparison as both products mature. The differences worth tracking: who hosts the artifact (Provn the platform vs. Helix the candidate-owned URL), what fills it (Provn challenge submissions vs. Helix-curated work history and AI-fluency artifacts), and who discovers the candidate through it (Provn employer search vs. inbound candidate sharing).

§ 08Risks & open questions

Where Provn might struggle, and what to watch:

  • Challenge authoring is high-touch. The Envorso challenge is excellent. Someone had to write it. Spring 2026 was 18 jobs across 5 partners — i.e., they're co-authoring per role with hiring managers, which doesn't scale per logo. If they push challenge authoring out to employers entirely, quality will vary and design effort becomes friction.
  • Roadmap collision with their own positioning. Listing resume / LinkedIn optimization on the roadmap contradicts the "we're against resume optimization" stance. How they resolve this messaging will say something about the discipline of the company.
